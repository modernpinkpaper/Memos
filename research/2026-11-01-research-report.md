## 2. Ikigai — what it really is, and where to learn more

**Found (most important):**
- The popular **four-circle Venn diagram** (what you love / are good at / world needs / can be paid for) is a **Western creation**, not Japanese. It does not appear in the original Japanese writing on ikigai.
- The Japanese psychiatrist **Mieko Kamiya** wrote the foundational book *Ikigai ni Tsuite* (1966). It is about finding meaning in daily life, not about a dream career.
- **Ken Mogi**, in *The Little Book of Ikigai*, describes ikigai as a set of daily habits of attention (start small, release yourself, harmony and sustainability, joy of little things, plus a fifth pillar the sources named inconsistently). Mogi himself rejects the Venn diagram.
- Dates and credit for the Venn diagram's origin differ between sources (names mentioned: Andrés Zuzunaga 2011, Marc Winn 2014). Treat these as unverified.

**Why this matters for you:** You said you "always chase something". The original idea of ikigai is the opposite: meaning found in small daily things, not one big purpose to achieve. Your walk, puzzles and cooking dinner for your family may already be ikigai.

**Schools or communities:** I did not find a formal school. There are many coaching sites and "Ikigai Tribe" style blogs; most are commercial. Best path is to read Kamiya (if you can find a translation), Mogi, and the Ikigai Tribe articles critically.

Sources:

- [Mieko Kamiya — Founder of Ikigai Psychology](https://finde-zukunft.de/english-blog/mieko-kamiya-nbspthe-founder-of-the-ikigai-psychology)

Five parts, and a 15-question tool (PERMA-Profiler, 3 questions per part) exists. ## 4. Your "skeleton path with checkpoints" for creative experiments

**Found:** Barbara Sher's Scanner Daybook is the closest published tool (one place for all projects). Nothing I found gives a start-middle-end template for hobby testing.

**Draft — "Try-It Skeleton" (a guideline, not a rigid plan):**

1. **START (5 minutes):** Write one sentence: *"I'm trying ___ to find out ___."* Choose a time box (suggest 2 weeks) and a small **finish line** (e.g., "one finished 15-second animation").
2. **Consumption filter:** Before learning or generating more, ask: *"What will I do with this?"* If no answer, stop.
3. **MIDDLE — Checkpoint 1 (about day 3–4):** Do I have anything made? Am I enjoying it? Y/N.
4. **MIDDLE — Checkpoint 2 (halfway):** Am I circling? If I did the same thing 3 times with no new output, change the question or stop.
5. **END (end of time box):** Decide one of: **Keep** (set a new finish line), **Park** (write down where I stopped), or **Kill** (one line on what I learned).
6. **Log it** in one notebook or note (Daybook idea). Max **2 active experiments** at once.

Why it fits you: you said aimless exploring leads to "worthless" feelings, but you do well with direction and an end goal.

## 5. Strength training you won't dread

**Found:**
- Health groups recommend at least **2 days a week** of muscle-strengthening for all major muscle groups.
- Studies in this area show strength gains from as little as 2 sessions per week, and even 1 per week can still help when total work is matched.
- A common simple plan: 2 full-body days, 2–3 sets per move, 6–12 reps, stopping 1–3 reps before failure.
- **Enjoyment evidence is weak.** Short studies suggest that letting people choose their own effort tends to improve how pleasant it feels and short-term sticking with it. No long-term study on women's strength training choice was found.
- Many "minimum dose" claims online (e.g. "20 minutes twice a week") could not be traced to a study.

**Draft ideas based on what you told me (you need constant mental activity, dislike it, like puzzles):**
- Pair it with something you like: a podcast or audiobook only during lifting.
- Keep it short: 2 days, 20–30 minutes, 5–6 moves. Same routine each time, so there are no decisions to make.
- Treat it like a puzzle: track weights/reps and try to beat last time ("level up" feeling).
- Attach it to the walk (e.g., right after walking, or right before).
- Choose the equipment: bodyweight, bands, dumbbells, or a gym.
- Since walking fixed your back, check with a doctor or physical therapist before heavy lifting if your back is a worry.

## 6. Raises for next year — "what is the ceiling?"

**Found (general HR practice, mostly blogs and public-sector policy; not legal or tax advice):**
- Use a simple **pay band** per role: a minimum and a maximum. Small companies don't need many grades.
- When someone reaches the top of the band, a common choice is a **one-time lump sum** equal to the raise they would have had, instead of raising base pay. This avoids compounding costs.
- Downsides: less motivating for top performers; may affect benefits tied to base pay; not guaranteed each year.
- Tell each person where they sit in their range.
- Check tax and benefits effects with an accountant. (One source claimed lump sums are taxed at 40%; I could not verify that.)

**Draft ideas for you:** Set a ceiling per role. Past the ceiling, offer other value (bonus, extra time off, better schedule, skills growth, a new role scope). This also fits your plan to document roles.

**I cannot give you real numbers** without your current pay, hours, and margins. See questions.

## 7. Cycle and mood tracking

**Found:**
- Doctors diagnose a severe form (PMDD) only by **daily symptom ratings across at least two cycles**. Memory alone is unreliable: about 60% of people who looked like PMDD on recall did not have it when daily ratings were checked.
- The standard tool is the **DRSP** (Daily Record of Severity of Problems): daily ratings 1–6.
- Symptoms usually start in the second half of the luteal phase (the roughly 12–14 days before the period) and ease within a few days after it starts.
- Treatments exist (for example SSRIs, sometimes only in the luteal phase, and hormonal options). A doctor decides.
- I found **no research specifically on "existential" distress** linked to the cycle. Hopelessness and despair are the nearest symptoms.

**Draft tracking (30 seconds a day):** Date, day of cycle (period start = day 1), mood 1–6, "existential/not enough" feeling 1–6, sleep, one-line note. Review after 2–3 cycles.

**Note:** If low days ever include thoughts of self-harm, or hurt your daily life, please speak to a doctor soon, not just track.

Sources:
- [IAPMD: What is PMDD?](https://iapmd.org/about-pmdd)
- [DRSP and diagnosis](https://faq.iapmd.org/en/articles/8232981-what-is-drsp-and-why-is-it-important-for-pmdd-diagnosis-and-research)
